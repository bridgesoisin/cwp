The Rite

The Order of Celebrating Matrimony

Everything below is drawn from the rites as the Church actually gives them — both the Order of Celebrating Matrimony and the traditional rite of the 1962 books. Read it once before your first meeting with a priest and you will be further ahead than most couples ever get.

I · The first decision

Which rite, and within Mass or outside it

This choice shapes everything after it — length, language, music, who may receive Communion, and how the day is timed. We plan all three.

Marriage within Mass

Ordo celebrandi Matrimonium intra Missam

The full nuptial Mass. Roughly seventy minutes. The consent sits after the homily, the nuptial blessing after the Our Father, and the whole rite is carried by the Eucharist. This is the normal form when both parties are Catholic.

Marriage outside Mass

Ordo celebrandi Matrimonium sine Missa

A complete and equally valid celebration of the sacrament: Liturgy of the Word, consent, rings, the nuptial blessing, the Our Father, dismissal. Roughly thirty-five minutes. Ordinarily used when one party is not Catholic — and often the more hospitable choice.

The traditional rite

Missa pro Sponso et Sponsa · 1962

The marriage rite of the Rituale Romanum followed by the nuptial Mass of the 1962 Missal, in Latin, ad orientem, sung to chant. The consent, the blessing of the ring, the gold and silver, and a nuptial blessing said over the bride kneeling at the altar after the Pater Noster.

Whether the traditional rite may be celebrated, and by whom, rests with the diocesan bishop under Traditionis custodes (2021), and practice differs considerably between Irish dioceses. We establish what is actually available to you — diocesan permission, a personal parish, or one of the traditional institutes — in writing, before any date is held. Nobody should book a hotel on a maybe.

There is no hierarchy of sincerity here. A marriage outside Mass is not a lesser wedding; in a mixed-religion marriage it is frequently the kinder one, because it does not divide the room at the Communion rail. We will talk this through with you and your celebrant before anything else is decided.

II · The modern rite, step by step

The Order of Celebrating Matrimony

Marked — yours where the rite leaves the choice to you. Everything else is fixed, and that is a mercy.

I

The Entrance Introitus — yours

The ministers, then the couple. You may process together, or be accompanied by your parents, or be met at the door by the priest. All three are provided for. Choose the entrance music now, not in the last month.

II

Greeting and Collect Collecta

Four collects are given for the wedding Mass. Ask your celebrant to read you all four; they differ in emphasis more than you would expect.

III

Liturgy of the Word Liturgia verbi — yours

A first reading, a responsorial psalm, a second reading, and the Gospel. The Gospel is always read by a deacon or priest; everything else may be read by family or friends. See the readings below.

IV

Homily

Give your celebrant something to work with. If he asks you how you met, tell him properly — the homily is the one part of the rite that can be about you by name.

V

The Questions before Consent Scrutinium

Three questions, asked of you both: freedom, faithfulness, and the acceptance of children. You answer them standing, in front of everyone. It is shorter and more moving than it sounds on paper.

VI

The Consent Consensus — yours

The sacrament itself. Two forms of words are provided, and either may be spoken by you or answered after the priest. Decide which at the rehearsal, out loud, standing up. Most couples change their minds once they hear it.

VII

Blessing and Giving of Rings Benedictio et traditio anulorum

Three blessings are provided. The rings may be carried by a child, but somebody sensible should have a spare pocket.

VIII

The Universal Prayer Oratio universalis — yours

The prayers of the faithful. Written by you, or with us, and the one place in the rite where you may name the people who could not be in the room.

IX

Liturgy of the Eucharist Liturgia eucharistica

At a nuptial Mass, the couple may bring up the gifts. A word in the worship aid about who may receive Communion saves a great deal of awkwardness in the aisle.

X

The Nuptial Blessing Benedictio nuptialis — yours

After the Our Father. Three forms are given, and they are genuinely different: one invokes the Holy Spirit at length, one is built on the women of scripture, one is brief and blunt. Read all three aloud before choosing.

XI

Marian Devotion Sub tuum praesidium — optional

Not part of the rite, and near-universal in Ireland: flowers laid at Our Lady’s altar while the schola sings. Allow it a full minute and a half. Do not let it be rushed for the photographer.

XII

Blessing and Dismissal Benedictio et dimissio

The solemn blessing, the dismissal, the signing of the register, and out. Have someone briefed to hold the back of the church for two minutes so you can leave to music rather than to a scrum.

III · The traditional rite

The Latin nuptial Mass, as the older books give it

The marriage rite of the Rituale Romanum, followed by the Missa pro Sponso et Sponsa of the 1962 Missal. Different in shape from the modern rite, not merely in language.

Two differences matter most. The marriage happens before the Mass begins — consent is exchanged at the altar rail, and only then does the priest go up to the altar and start the Introit. And the nuptial blessing falls after the Pater Noster, with the couple kneeling on the sanctuary step, in a prayer that names Rachel, Rebecca and Sara and asks that they see their children’s children to the third and fourth generation.

I

The Address Ad sponsum et sponsam

The priest meets the couple at the altar rail and addresses them: “Dear children of God, you are come today to seal your love for each other before God…” No Mass has begun. The church is simply watching a promise being made.

II

The Interrogation Interrogatio

Each is asked by name, the man first, standing at the bride’s right: do you freely and willingly take her, here present, as your lawful wife according to the laws of God and of holy Church? — I do.

III

The Consent Consensus

Right hands joined, each repeating after the priest: to have and to hold from this day forward, for better, for worse, for richer, for poorer, in sickness and in health, till death do us part. Then the priest, over their joined hands: Ego conjúngo vos in matrimónium, in nómine Patris, ✞ et Fílii, et Spíritus Sancti.

IV

Blessing of the Ring Benedictio anuli

One ring is blessed and sprinkled with holy water, then given: in the name of the Father at the thumb, and of the Son at the index, and of the Holy Ghost at the middle finger, Amen as it comes to rest on the ring finger. A second ring for the groom is a later custom and is easily provided for.

V

The Gold and Silver Arrhae — optional

The groom places coins in the bride’s hands: “This gold and silver I give you, tokens of all my worldly goods.” Old, beautiful, and almost always omitted because nobody mentions it. Worth reviving; the coins become an heirloom.

VI

Psalm and Prayers Confirma hoc, Deus

Versicles at the altar — Confirma hoc, Deus, quod operátus es in nobis — the Kyrie, a silent Pater Noster, and the collect Réspice, quaésumus, Dómine. The marriage is now complete. The Mass follows as its crown, not its container.

VII

The Nuptial Mass begins Introíbo ad altáre Dei

The priest goes up to the altar and begins the prayers at the foot — I will go in unto the altar of God — while the choir sings the Introit. The couple kneel at a prie-dieu within the sanctuary, closer to the altar than at any other Mass of their lives.

VIII

The Nuptial Blessing Propitiáre, Dómine — the heart of it

After the Pater Noster and before the Libera nos, the priest turns from the Epistle corner and prays over the kneeling couple: that she be dear to her husband like Rachel, wise like Rebecca, long-lived and faithful like Sara; that theirs be a yoke of love and peace; and that they see their children’s children to the third and fourth generation. It is the oldest nuptial text the Roman rite has.

IX

The Final Blessing Deus Abraham, Deus Isaac

Before the last blessing the priest turns once more to the couple, kneeling: May the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob be with you. Then the blessing of all, and the Last Gospel — the prologue of St John, Et Verbum caro factum est, at which everyone genuflects.

The propers

Missa pro Sponso et Sponsa

Unlike the modern rite, the texts are not chosen — they are given, and they are the same for every couple who has ever been married in this Mass.

Deus Israël conjúngat vos Tobias 7:15, 8:19 · Ps 127:1 — May the God of Israel join you together Introit
Wives, be subject to your husbands Ephesians 5:22–33 — the great text on marriage as the union of Christ and the Church Epistle
Uxor tua sicut vitis abúndans Psalm 127:3 — Thy wife as a fruitful vine on the sides of thy house Gradual
Mittat vobis Dóminus auxílium Psalm 19:3 — May the Lord send you help from the sanctuary Alleluia
What God hath joined together Matthew 19:3–6 Gospel
In te sperávi, Dómine Psalm 30:15–16 Offertory
Ecce sic benedicétur homo Psalm 127:4–6 — Behold, thus shall the man be blessed that feareth the Lord Communion

The Ordinary

Missa de Angelis is the setting most congregations can actually sing, and the one we start from. A schola carries the propers above it; for a larger building, a polyphonic Ordinary over chanted propers.

Veil and mantilla

Customary rather than required, and worth deciding early so the bridesmaids know. The rubric that the bride’s hand is uncovered, and a widow’s covered, is a real one and still in the book.

The booklet is not optional

At a Latin Mass the worship aid is the guest experience. Facing Latin and English, the postures marked — sit, kneel, stand — and the Ordinary printed where it falls. Guests who have never seen this Mass can follow every word.

IV · The readings

Nine, seven, fourteen, ten

The Lectionary gives nine first readings, seven psalms, fourteen second readings and ten gospels for a wedding. Here are the ones couples reach for, and the ones we quietly push them towards.

Frequently chosen

A reading from the Song of Songs Song 2:8–10, 14, 16; 8:6–7 First

Love is always patient and kind 1 Corinthians 12:31 – 13:8 Second
This is my commandment John 15:9–12 Gospel

Worth a second look

The marriage of Tobiah and Sarah Tobit 8:4–8 First
A worthy wife is her husband’s joy Sirach 26:1–4, 13–16 First
I will make a new covenant Jeremiah 31:31–32a, 33–34a First
Offer your living bodies as a sacrifice Romans 12:1–2, 9–18 Second
The house built on rock Matthew 7:21, 24–29 Gospel

A note on 1 Corinthians 13: it is beautiful and it is not about marriage. Paul is writing to a fractious congregation about how to live together. That does not disqualify it — but if you choose it, choose it knowing what it is, and ask your celebrant to say so in the homily. It lands twice as hard.

V · Sacred music

Music that belongs in a church

Most parishes have a written music policy. Almost no couple is shown it. Here is roughly where we start, moment by moment.

Rorate caeli / Veni sponsa mea Gregorian chant, proper to the season Entrance
Missa Papae Marcelli — Kyrie Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina Ordinary
Ubi caritas Maurice Duruflé Offertory
O magnum mysterium Tomás Luis de Victoria Offertory
Ave verum corpus W. A. Mozart, K. 618 Communion
Panis angelicus César Franck Communion
Ave Maria Franz Biebl, or the chant Marian devotion
Toccata from Symphony No. 5 Charles-Marie Widor Recessional

What parishes usually will not permit. Secular love songs during the rite, recorded backing tracks, and anything from a musical. This is not snobbery: the rule is that music in the liturgy must be liturgical. Save the Van Morrison for the drinks reception, where it will be better received anyway.

What we arrange. A cantor and organist as a minimum; a schola of six to eight for polyphony; strings where the building can carry it. We agree the programme with the parish director of music in writing, and we pay the musicians. You should never be handling that on the morning.

VI · Preparation

The paperwork, in plain terms

Marrying in Ireland means satisfying two authorities at once — the Church and the State — and they want different things on different timetables. This is the part we take off you entirely.

Contact the parish as early as you can, and certainly before you book a venue. Most Irish parishes ask for at least three months’ notice; popular churches are taken eighteen months out. The priest who marries you need not be the parish priest, but the parish priest of the church must delegate him in writing.

A formal interview, conducted separately with each of you by a priest, establishing that you are free to marry and that you understand what you are consenting to. It produces the pre-nuptial enquiry form, which travels with the rest of your file to the diocese of the church.

It sounds forbidding and is usually a pleasant conversation over an hour.

A recently issued long-form baptismal certificate for each Catholic party — issued within the last six months, from the church of baptism, with all annotations. A confirmation certificate. Letters of freedom for any diocese you have lived in for more than six months since the age of eighteen. A certificate of completion of a marriage preparation course.

The baptismal certificate is the one that catches people out. Request it early; parish offices are not fast.

Book it the moment you have a date — places go months ahead, particularly around Dublin, and you will need the certificate before your file can be completed.

A marriage between a Catholic and a baptised non-Catholic requires permission for a mixed marriage; between a Catholic and an unbaptised person it requires a dispensation from disparity of cult. Both are routine, both are granted by the diocese, and your celebrant applies for them as part of the file.

The Catholic party undertakes to do what they can to raise any children in the faith. The other party is simply informed of that undertaking — nothing is required of them. In these cases we usually recommend marriage outside Mass.

You must give at least three months’ notice of intention to marry, in person, to a Registrar of Civil Marriages, and you must both attend that appointment. You will be issued a Marriage Registration Form, which must be given to your celebrant before the wedding and returned, signed, afterwards.

Without the Marriage Registration Form the ceremony cannot be civilly registered. We diary the notice appointment for you and check the form is in the sacristy the day before.

Start with the celebrant, not the church. You need a priest who offers the older rite and who may do so in the diocese where you intend to marry — whether that is a diocesan priest with the bishop’s permission, a personal parish, or one of the traditional institutes. Since Traditionis custodes in 2021 that permission is the diocesan bishop’s to give, and Irish dioceses differ a good deal in practice.

We make that enquiry for you and get the answer in writing before you hold a date or pay a venue deposit. The canonical and civil paperwork is otherwise identical to any other Catholic wedding — same pre-nuptial enquiry, same certificates, same three-month notice to the State.

Then the booklet does the work. Facing columns of Latin and English, every posture marked, the Ordinary printed where it actually falls rather than in an appendix, and a short note at the front explaining what is about to happen and why the couple kneel where they do.

Done properly, guests with no Latin and no Mass in fifteen years follow the whole thing. Done badly — or not at all — they spend an hour wondering when to stand.

Ordinarily a Catholic marriage takes place in a parish church. Permission to marry elsewhere — a monastery church, an oratory, a college chapel — is given by the bishop and is often straightforward. Permission for a hotel or a garden is not ordinarily given at all.

If a particular building matters to you, tell us at the first meeting. It is much easier to build a wedding around a church than to move one later.

This page is a practical summary, not canon law, and dioceses differ in detail. Your celebrant and the diocesan office have the final word — and we will be the ones asking them.
