The journal · Paperwork

The paperwork, in the order you actually need it

8 min read Ireland

Marrying in Ireland means satisfying two authorities that do not talk to each other, want different documents, and run on different clocks. Only one of them will chase you, and it is not the one with the legal deadline.

The two files

Everything below belongs to one of two piles, and confusing them is the single most common cause of panic at four weeks out.

  • The church file. Held by your parish, sent on to the diocese of the church where you are marrying. Built from the pre-nuptial enquiry, your certificates and your course. Sometimes called the green book.
  • The State file. Held by the civil registrar. Built from one appointment, and it produces the Marriage Registration Form — the single piece of paper without which the ceremony cannot be registered.

They are separate. Completing one does nothing for the other. A priest cannot give you the State's form, and a registrar has no interest in your baptismal certificate.

The only hard legal deadline

You must give the civil registrar at least three months' notice of your intention to marry, in person, with both of you present. Not three months from when you ring — three months from the appointment itself, and appointments book out. Treat this as the first thing you diary and the last thing you leave late. Everything else on this page can be recovered from; this one cannot.

The order to do it in

Twelve to ten months out

Ring the parish and confirm the church and the celebrant. Then, in the same week, do the two slow things: book the registrar appointment, and request your long-form baptismal certificate from the church where you were baptised. It must normally be issued within the last six months, with all annotations, and parish offices are not fast — three or four weeks is ordinary, longer if the parish has amalgamated or the register is in an archive.

Book your marriage preparation course now as well. Accord runs most of them in Ireland and places go months ahead, particularly around Dublin.

Nine to six months out

Sit down with a priest for the pre-nuptial enquiry. It is a formal interview conducted with each of you separately, establishing that you are free to marry and understand what you are consenting to. It sounds forbidding and is almost always a pleasant hour.

Gather what goes with it: your confirmation certificate, and a letter of freedom for every diocese you have lived in for more than six months since turning eighteen. The letters catch out anyone who has worked abroad — a year in London or Sydney means writing to that diocese, and that takes time.

Six months out

If either of you is not Catholic, this is when the permission or dispensation is applied for. A marriage between a Catholic and a baptised non-Catholic needs permission for a mixed marriage; between a Catholic and an unbaptised person, a dispensation from disparity of cult. Both are routine, both come from the diocese, and your celebrant applies for them as part of the file. Nothing is required of the non-Catholic party beyond being informed.

If you are marrying outside your own parish, the parish priest of the church must delegate your celebrant in writing. This is a five-minute administrative act that is occasionally forgotten until the week before.

Three months out

Attend the registrar appointment, both of you, in person. You will be issued the Marriage Registration Form. Put it somewhere it cannot be lost, and give it to your celebrant before the day — it is signed after the ceremony and returned for registration.

The week before

Confirm with the sacristy that the church file is complete and that the Marriage Registration Form is physically in the building. Ask plainly. This is a thirty-second conversation that prevents the only genuinely unrecoverable problem on this page.

The two that catch everybody

The baptismal certificate. Not the one in your mother's drawer. A newly issued long-form copy from the church of baptism, with annotations. Request it early.

The registrar appointment. Three months is a legal minimum, not a target, and the appointment itself has a waiting list. Couples who leave it until they have chosen a venue have already lost a month.

If you were already married civilly

A convalidation is a true celebration of the sacrament, not a blessing of what happened before. The requirements are much the same, plus your civil marriage certificate. It can be as small as four people in a side chapel or as full as any nuptial Mass.

What this page is and is not

A practical summary for planning, written from current Irish practice. Dioceses differ in detail and procedures change. Your celebrant and the diocesan office have the final word — and if you engage us, we are the ones asking them, and tracking every item above to completion.
