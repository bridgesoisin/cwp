The budget guide

A wedding you can afford. A marriage you cannot price.

The sacrament costs almost nothing. Everything around it is a choice. Here is where the money actually goes in Ireland, what moves the number most, and the twelve things we would cut first — along with the five we would never touch.

Where it goes Twelve levers What not to cut The countdown

I · The shape of it

Where the money actually goes

One category is two-thirds of most Irish weddings. Every hour spent agonising over the others is an hour spent on the wrong problem.

II · The one number

The guest list is the budget

Almost every line above multiplies by headcount. Change this number and you have changed the wedding; change the flowers and you have changed the flowers.

Sixty

Everyone knows everyone

Comfortable total, near enough€16,000

  • A long table rather than a room of rounds
  • Real conversation with every guest
  • Venues that would otherwise be out of reach
  • The hardest list to write, and the best day

A hundred and twenty

The default, rarely chosen

Comfortable total, near enough€28,000

  • Most Irish couples land here without deciding to
  • You will not speak properly to a third of the room
  • Every supplier prices in bands around this number
  • Worth arriving at on purpose, if you arrive at all

Two hundred

A parish, not a party

Comfortable total, near enough€42,000

  • Genuinely right for some families — and some parishes
  • Choose it, rather than letting it happen
  • Consider a bigger church and a simpler meal
  • The ceremony scales beautifully; dinner does not

One honest conversation is worth more than every saving on this page: sit down with both sets of parents early, say what you can spend, and ask plainly what — if anything — they intend to contribute. Doing it in month one prevents the two arguments that otherwise arrive in month five.

III · Tips and tricks

Twelve levers, and what each one costs you

Nothing here is free. Each saves real money and takes something in exchange — usually time, or somebody's goodwill. Both are stated.

01

Bring your own wine

Saves €800–2,000. Ask every venue for its corkage rate in the first email, before you fall in love with the room — some charge nothing, some charge more than the wine. Costs you: an afternoon buying by the case, and someone to deliver it.

02

Cut the guest list before anything else

Saves €90 a head, everywhere at once. Twenty fewer guests is close to two thousand euro and a materially better day. Costs you: the hardest conversations of the whole process. Have them early, together, and only once.

03

Marry off-peak

Saves 15–30% on the venue. A Friday, a Sunday, or anything between November and March. Winter weddings in a candlelit church are the most beautiful we do. Costs you: some guests will find travel harder; say so on the save-the-date.

04

A playlist instead of a DJ

Saves €500–900. Build it over months, not the week before; three hours minimum, ordered, with the slow songs deliberately placed. Costs you: somebody sober minding a laptop, and no one to read the room at midnight.

05

A phone on a tripod instead of a videographer

Saves €1,200–2,500. One locked-off camera at the back of the church, recording the whole rite, is worth more in ten years than most highlight reels. Costs you: no edit, no second angle, and someone must remember to press record.

06

Move the church flowers to the venue

Saves €400–800. The same arrangements do both jobs if somebody is briefed to lift them during the drinks reception. Costs you: two people, a car, and the sacristan's blessing — ask first.

07

Let the family make the cake

Saves €300–450. An aunt who bakes is a better cake and a better story. Costs you: the nerve to ask, and a plan for getting it there intact.

08

Digital save-the-dates

Saves €200–400. Send the date by email or message and spend the paper budget on the invitation and the Mass booklet instead, where guests actually keep it. Costs you: almost nothing. Do it.

09

Fewer, better courses

Saves €8–15 a head. Three excellent courses beat five adequate ones, and nobody has ever left an Irish wedding hungry. Costs you: a conversation with a chef who would rather upsell.

10

Borrow, or buy once worn

Saves €800–2,000. A dress worn once by someone else is still a dress worn once. Budget for alterations either way — that is where an inexpensive dress is won or lost. Costs you: time, and letting go of the shop.

11

Skip the favours

Saves €300–600. They are left on the table. Every time. If you want to give something, give the booklet. Costs you: nothing whatsoever.

12

Ask for help, specifically

Saves €1,000+ across the day. People want to help and will not volunteer. Hand each person one named job with a deadline — the list is below. Costs you: the discipline to hand it over and then not check.

IV · Hold the line

Five things we would not economise on

Cut anything else first. These five are the difference between a cheap wedding and a wedding done cheaply, which are not the same thing at all.

The offering to your celebrant

There is no fee for a sacrament and there never will be. There is an offering, and it should be generous. He gave you months of preparation, a morning in the sacristy, and the homily he wrote for you specifically.

The singers

Musicians are asked to work for nothing more often than any other trade at a wedding. Pay the organist, pay the cantor, pay the schola, and pay them before the day rather than after it.

The Mass booklet

The cheapest meaningful thing on the whole budget. It is what lets half a room who have never been to Mass follow what is happening — and the only item guests take home and keep.

The photographer

Everything else is gone by Sunday. Book the best you can afford, brief them properly on what may and may not happen in a sanctuary, and take fewer hours rather than a cheaper photographer.

The preparation

The course, the conversations, the retreat. You are preparing for a marriage, not an event, and this is the only line on the budget that is still paying you back in twenty years.

One good meal

Not five courses — one genuinely good meal, served warm, at a sensible hour. It is the single thing guests discuss on the way home, and the one economy they notice.

V · The countdown

What to do, and when to stop doing it

Counted backwards from the day, so it works whatever your date. Twelve months is comfortable. Six is entirely enough — we have done it more than once.

T−12

The date, the church, the priest

In that order, and before anything else is booked. Confirm the church is free and that your celebrant is free. Gather venue quotations in writing — hire fee, accommodation rates, dinner and drinks packages, and the corkage rate. Draft a rough guest list to get a headcount. Draft a budget, then have the conversation with both sets of parents.

T−10

Paperwork and preparation, started early

Book the appointment to give notice to the civil registrar — the State requires at least three months, and appointments themselves book out, so treat this as a hard deadline with a long runway. Enrol on a marriage preparation course; places go months ahead around Dublin. Request your baptismal certificate now: parish offices are not fast.

T−9

Book the venue. Delegate everything else.

Confirm the reservation. Send save-the-dates, digital or printed. Hand out the named jobs below — this is the month that decides whether you enjoy the next eight. Begin looking for a dress and suit, and decide whether you want an afters dress.

T−6

The parish file, the music, the betrothal

Sit down with your parish priest and work through the pre-nuptial enquiry — the parish paperwork, sometimes called the green book — and check whether your baptismal certificate needs stamping. Decide the Mass music and contact the choir. Settle DJ or playlist. Design the invitations. And ask your priest about a betrothal — see below.

T−4

Honeymoon, fittings, and the booklet

Book flights and accommodation. Schedule dress and suit fittings. Choose the first dance and any parent dances. Hand the Mass booklet and the day's running order to whoever is designing them — they need longer than you think, and the booklet should be finished a fortnight early, not the night before.

T−3

Invitations out, tasting done

Post the invitations. Do the food tasting. Reconfirm flowers, cake and decorations in writing. Decide the offerings for the priest and the choir now, while there is money in the account, and put them in named envelopes with somebody sensible.

T−6 wks

Numbers, speeches, rehearsal

Give the venue a final headcount — most want it well in advance. Confirm the ceremony details: music, booklet, choir, readers. Write and actually rehearse the speeches, out loud, standing up. Order thank-you cards now and address them while you still have time. Book the church rehearsal.

T−2 wks

Walk the room, pack the bag

Final fittings. A walkthrough with the venue manager covering seating, décor and timings. Assemble the day kit — tissues, plasters, safety pins, a sewing kit, snacks, a phone charger. Pack for the honeymoon and check the passports. Then stop.

T−1 wk

The week, and the day

Collect the attire. Confirm hair, make-up and nails. Hold the rehearsal. Spend an evening with the people who raised you. Pray together the night before, and sleep. On the morning, the only thing that has to go right is the vow — everything else is somebody else's job, and you gave them that job months ago.

VI · Hand it over

Nine jobs, nine names

Write a name beside each one. Not “the girls” — a name, and a date. People are glad to be asked and hopeless at volunteering.

  • The hen and the stag — hand this over entirely and do not ask questions
  • The cake — who bakes it, who transports it, who cuts it
  • Flowers — including moving them from church to venue
  • Invitations — design, addressing, posting, and chasing replies
  • Church and venue décor — and clearing it afterwards
  • The Mass booklet — the one to give your most exacting friend
  • Seating — give it to whoever knows the family politics
  • Music — church choir, and the playlist or DJ for the afters
  • Cars and buses — getting guests there, and home again

VII · Free

The best of it costs nothing

Three things couples tell us afterwards they would not trade — and not one of them appears on any budget.

A betrothal

Ritus sponsalium

Ask your priest about a betrothal after a Sunday Mass: a blessing at which you formally pledge, before the parish, to marry each other. It is a real promise with real weight in the Church, not a rehearsal. Almost nobody in Ireland knows it is still available. It costs nothing, takes five minutes, and couples describe it as the moment the engagement became serious.

A silent retreat

A weekend each, separately, some months before. Most Irish retreat houses ask only a modest offering. You will spend a year being asked about napkins; this is the one part of the preparation that is actually about the marriage.

The night before

Pray together, with your families if they will. Go to bed early. There is nothing left that an extra hour will fix, and the couples who did this are the ones who remember the morning clearly.

Perfection is not the point, and it is not achievable in any case. Something will go wrong on the day — it always does, and it is never the thing you worried about. What you are actually doing takes about ninety seconds and cannot be improved by any amount of money.
